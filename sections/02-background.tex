Being able to track position has become increasingly important, and is something that is often taken for granted.
We are used to being able to tell where we are at all times and the most common way to do this is through the use of GPS.
However GPS are not always available, and there are many situations where GPS is not a viable option.
For example if you are inside a building or underground, GPS signals may not be able to reach you.
In these situations it's of interest to look at alternative methods for tracking position.

GPS, global positioning system, is a satellite-based navigation system that allows users to determine their exact location anywhere on Earth.
GPS was originally developed by the United States Department of Defense for military purposes, but has since been made available for civilian use \cite{GPS}.
The system consists of a network of satellites that orbit the Earth and transmits two signals of a wavelength around 19-24 cm to GPS receivers on the ground.
Based on the time it takes for the signals to reach the receiver, the receiver can calculate its distance from each satellite and use this information to determine its location.
The system is highly accurate and can provide location information with an accuracy of a few meters.
The GPS system is however not fit for determining the position inside of a building as the signals from the satellites are often blocked by walls and other obstacles.

One method for determining position indoor is presented by Sheinker et al. \cite{sheinker2013localization}, they present a method for 2D localization using beacons of low frequency, quasi-static magnetic fields.
Low frequency magnetic fields are able to penetrate foliage, soil, walls and other obstacles making it a suitable choice when line of sight methods are not viable.
Each beacon consists of a coil driven by an alternating current at a frequency that is unique to that beacon, generating a magnetic field that can be detected by a receiver. 
The object that is to be detected is equipped with a biaxial search coil that measures the magnetic field generated by the beacons. 
Since each beacon transmits its own frequency a range of lock-in amplifiers are used to extract the amplitude and phase from the signal and used to filter out magnetic background noise. 

Based on this principle the authors presents two methods of localization. 
The first method utilizes a pair of co-located beacons oriented perpendicular to each other, each transmitting at a different frequency.
By measuring the resulting components of the magnetic field the position of the reciever can be calculated.
The second method utilized a couple of beacons that are spaced apart and orientated in the same direction, each transmitting at a different frequency.
If the beacons are placed apart it's not necessary to know the magnetic moments of the beacons , as long as the magnetic moments are equal.
The magnetic moments are described as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{I}
\end{equation}
Where $\mathbf{A}$ is the area of the coil, $\mathbf{N}$ is the number of turns in the coil, and $\mathbf{I}$ is the current through the coil.
The vector $\mathbf{M}$ is the magnetic moment of the coil and is oriented in the direction of the coils axis.

Another approach to indoor localization is presented by De Angelis et al. \cite{deangelis2015indoor}, they also utilize a low frequency alternating current (AC), but instead of a single receiver detecting mutiple beacons they use coplanar resonant coils and is based on recieved power measurements.
Resonance makes the recieved signal stronger so it can be detected from farther away. It also helps filter out signals at other frequencies.

The system designed is based on a network of transmitter nodes, each node consists of an oscillator circuit and of a resonating coil.
The system also contains a receiver node, which position is to be estimated.
When the transmitter nodes are powered they generate a magnetic field that interacts with the receiver node, inducing a voltage in the receiver coil.
The authors show that by measuring the root-means-square voltage of the receiver coil it's possible to relate the voltage to the distance between the known position of the transmitter nodes and the unknown position of the receiver node.
When the transmitter and receiver nodes are coplanar, meaning that there exists a plane that contains the axes of all the coils, the inductance depends only on the distance separating them. 
This makes it possible to estimate the position using linear fitting of the measured voltage to known voltages at known distances.
The system must be calibrated first. The reciever is placed at different known distances from each transmitter. The measured voltages are used to make a calibration curve. Then the system uses this curve to estimate the distance from recieved voltage.

With three or more distance measurments can a position in 2D be find. Each distance creates a circle around a transmitter and the reciever should be where the circles meet. Measurement errors can disturb the circles from meeting at one point, so therefore they used a least-square method to find the position that best represented all the measured distances.

The system was tested using coils with a radius of 9 cm operating at a frequancy of 189.5kHz.
With the transmitter nodes placed in a triangular formation in tree corners of a 3x3 $m^2$ area and the reciever placed in a 1.2x1.2 $m^2$ area in the center of the triangle, the authors were able to achieve a localization error with an average of 3.3 cm.
With less favroble conditions, and the reciever placed outside the triangle, the average localization error increased to 12.7 cm. This shows that placement has a large effect on accuracy. The best results are achieved when the fixed nodes surrounded the moving node. 

The transmitter and reciever roles can also be reversed, and could be used for our railway system. Each train could carry a small transmitter and fixed reciever coils could be placed under the table. The same calibration and positioning method could then be used to find the train's position.




\chapter{Background Analysis} \label{sec:background-analysis}

\section{Coil-Based Electromagnetic Positioning Systems}\label{sec:bg-1-1}
\subsection{How It Works}\label{sec:bg-1-1-1}

Coil-based positioning systems use magnetic fields to determine the position of an object. When an alternating 
current goes through a transmitter coil it produces a changing magnetic field. This field can induce a voltage 
in a nearby receiver coil. The received signal depends on the distance and orientation between the coils. 
By comparing measurements from coils at known positions it is possible to estimate the location of the unknown 
coil \cite{deangelis2015indoor}.



\subsection{Existing Systems}\label{sec:bg-1-1-2}

De Angelis et al. \cite{deangelis2015indoor} developed an indoor positioning system using resonant transmitter and 
receiver coils. Their main system used fixed transmitters and a moving receiver. The received voltage was used 
to estimate distances between the coils. These distances were then used to calculate the position through 
trilateration.

The writer described two modes. In \textbf{time separation mode} the transmitters operate one after 
another. In \textbf{frequency separation mode} several transmitters operate at the same time using different 
frequencies. This allows the receiver to identify each transmitter. Their improved system used frequency separation 
and a Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) to separate the received signals.

Sheinker et al. \cite{sheinker2013localization} developed another positioning system using fixed transmitters and 
a magnetic receiver. Their system also used different frequencies to identify the transmitters. They 
calculated the position directly from magnetic field measurements by using a mathematical model instead of first 
estimating distances.

Both papers gives useful methods for electromagnetic positioning. In the our train system 
the arrangement will be reversed. Each train will carry a transmitter coil while the receiver coils will be 
fixed under the table.




\section{Theory}\label{sec:bg-1-2}
\subsection{Magnetic Field of a Coil}\label{sec:bg-1-2-1}

A electric current produces a magnetic field around a wire. The strength and direction of this field can be 
described using Biot--Savart's law \cite{sadiku2018elements}.

When the wire is formed into a coil the magnetic fields produced by its turns combine. The resulting field depends 
on the current and the shape of the coil. It also depends on the number of turns. If an alternating current flows 
through the coil the magnetic field changes with time.

The field around a circular current loop is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bg-a01} (Appendix~\ref{app:bg-figures}).



\subsection{Magnetic Dipole} \label{sec:bg-1-2-2}

A coil carrying current can be represented as a magnetic dipole when the distance from the coil to the measurement point is much larger than
the physical size of the coil, such as its diameter \cite{sadiku2018elements}. This simplifies the calculation of the magnetic field around the coil.

The magnitude of the magnetic dipole moment is:

\begin{equation}
    m = NIA
\end{equation}

where $m$ is the magnetic dipole moment
($\mathrm{A\,m^2}$), $N$ is the number of turns,
$I$ is the current (A) and $A$ is the coil area
($\mathrm{m^2}$).

The magnetic dipole model also describes how the magnetic field changes with distance and direction.
According to Sadiku \cite{sadiku2018elements}, the magnetic flux density can be expressed as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{B}(r,\theta) =
    \frac{\mu_0 m}{4\pi r^3}
    \left(
        2\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}
        +
        \sin\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
    \right)
\end{equation}

where $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space, $r$ is the distance from the coil centre to the
measurement point and $\theta$ is the angle measured from the magnetic dipole axis. The unit vectors
$\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ represent the radial and angular directions.

it shows that the magnetic field decreases with the third power of distance when the observation
direction remains unchanged. It also shows that the field depends on the direction relative to the coil axis.

The magnetic dipole field is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:bg-a02}
(Appendix~\ref{app:bg-figures}).





\subsection{Magnetic Flux and Faraday's Law of Induction}\label{sec:bg-1-2-3}

Magnetic flux describes the magnetic field passing through a surface. For a receiver coil it depends on the magnetic field strength and how the coil is oriented relative to the field \cite{sadiku2018elements}.

The magnetic flux is expressed as:

\[
\Phi = \int_S \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S}
\]

where \(\Phi\) is the magnetic flux (Wb), B is the magnetic flux density (T) and dS represents an element of the surface area.

According to Faraday's law a changing magnetic flux induces a voltage in a coil:

\[
V_{\mathrm{ind}} = -N\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t}
\]

Here V is the induced voltage and N is the number of turns. The negative sign describes the direction of the induced voltage.




\subsection{Mutual Inductance and Magnetic Coupling}\label{sec:bg-1-2-4}

When the magnetic field from one coil passes through another coil the two coils are magnetically coupled. Mutual inductance describes the strength of 
this interaction \cite{sadiku2018elements}.

It can be expressed as:

\[
M = \frac{N_{\mathrm r}\Phi_{\mathrm r}}{I_{\mathrm t}}
\]

where M is the mutual inductance (H), \(N_{\mathrm r}\) is the number of receiver turns, \(\Phi_{\mathrm r}\) is the magnetic flux through one receiver 
turn caused by the transmitter and \(I_{\mathrm t}\) is the transmitter current.

Mutual inductance depends on the coil dimensions and their relative positions. It also changes when the coils are rotated. These changes affect the 
voltage induced in the receiver coil.

The interaction between two circuits is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:bg-a04} (Appendix~\ref{app:bg-figures}).





\section{Transmitter and Receiver Coil}\label{sec:bg-1-3}

\subsection{Coil Parameters and Signal}\label{sec:bg-1-3-1}

The electromagnetic principles in Section~\ref{sec:bg-1-2} show that the received voltage is affected by several coil parameters. These include the coil 
size and number of turns.
The transmitter current also affects the signal. A higher current produces a stronger magnetic field and can induce a larger voltage in the receiver.

The operating frequency decides how quickly the magnetic field changes. According to Faraday's law a faster change in magnetic flux can produce a large 
induced voltage. The received signal is strongest when the coils are near their resonant frequency. The signal becomes weaker when the frequency moves 
away from resonance. \cite{deangelis2015indoor,sadiku2018elements}.





\subsection{Coil Distance and Orientation} \label{sec:bg-1-3-2}

Mutual inductance depends on the distance and orientation between two coils. De Angelis et al. \cite{deangelis2015indoor} studied this using 
a model of circular coils. They found that in a coplanar arrangement, mutual inductance depends only on the distance between the coil centres.

In the model train system the transmitters move above the fixed receivers. Both horizontal distance
and vertical separation can therefore affect the received voltage.





\subsection{Resonance and Quality Factor}\label{sec:bg-1-3-3}

De Angelis et al. \cite{deangelis2015indoor} used resonant circuits to increase the signal. A resonant circuit replys  strongly near its resonant frequency.

An important parameter is the quality factor (Q). For the resonator model used in \cite{deangelis2015indoor} it is expressed as:

\[
Q = \frac{\omega L}{R}
\]

where \(\omega\) is the angular frequency (rad/s), L is the inductance (H) and R is the resistance (\(\Omega\)).

A higher quality factor can increase the received signal near resonance. But it also makes the frequency bandwidth narrower.

De Angelis et al. \cite{deangelis2015indoor} measured a resonant frequency of approximately 189.7 kHz and a bandwidth of about 3 kHz. Their measured 
frequency response is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bg-a07} (Appendix~\ref{app:bg-figures}).

The transmitter frequencies must be different so the signals can be identified. They must also be close to resonance so the signals stay strong. 
This is important when several model trains need to be identified at the same time.






\section{Signal Detection and Identification}\label{sec:bg-1-4}

\subsection{Amplification and Filtering} \label{sec:bg-1-4-1}

The voltage received by a coil can be very weak. Amplification makes the signal easier to measure.
De Angelis et al. \cite{deangelis2015indoor} used an amplifier with a gain of 100.
They found that strong signals from nearby coils could overload the amplifier and cause errors.

Filtering helps reduce noise and unwanted signals. 


\subsection{Frequency-Based Identification} \label{sec:bg-1-4-2}

Each transmitter can use a different frequency so the receiver can identify its signal.

De Angelis et al. \cite{deangelis2015indoor} tested this method with four transmitters.
They used a Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) to separate the signals by frequency as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bg-a08}
(Appendix~\ref{app:bg-figures}).
They were able to identify all four signals but also found that some frequencies could cause interference.




\subsection{Lock-In Detection}\label{sec:bg-1-4-3}

Lock-in detection is another method for measuring signals at known frequencies. It is especially useful when the wanted signal is weak compared with 
unwanted signals or noise.

The method uses a reference signal at the frequency of the transmitter. The received signal is multiplied by this reference. When the frequencies match, 
then the result contains a slowly changing component related to the wanted signal. A low-pass filter removes the higher-frequency components. This makes it possible to estimate the wanted signal amplitude.

For example, if two transmitters operate at different frequencies a lock-in detector can be tuned to the first transmitter. A second detector can then 
measure the signal from the other transmitter.

Sheinker et al. \cite{sheinker2013localization} used this approach with a two-axis magnetic sensor and four lock-in amplifiers. Two amplifiers measured 
the field components from the first transmitter and two measured the components from the second transmitter. Their receiver structure is shown in 
Figure~\ref{fig:bg-a09} (Appendix~\ref{app:bg-figures}).

The authors also used phase recovery to track changes in the signal phase. This allowed them to obtain both amplitude and phase information for 
positioning.

DFT-based processing and lock-in detection therefore provide two ways to separate transmitter signals. The DFT analyses several frequencies together 
while lock-in detection extracts selected frequency components. Both methods are relevant to identifying multiple trains.





\section{2D Position Estimation}\label{sec:bg-1-5}

\subsection{Voltage-Based Estimation}\label{sec:bg-1-5-1}

After the transmitter signals have been separated their measured amplitudes can be used for position estimation.

One approach is to estimate the distance between the coils using the received voltage. This is possible because the magnetic coupling becomes 
weaker as the distance increases. 
The received voltage also depends on the coil orientation and electrical properties such as resistance and inductance.

De Angelis et al. \cite{deangelis2015indoor} used voltage-based distance estimation. Their method required calibration to determine the relationship 
between the measured voltage and distance.





\subsubsection{Calibration} \label{sec:bg-1-5-1-1}

De Angelis et al. \cite{deangelis2015indoor} measured the received voltage at known distances from each transmitter. The distances ranged from
1.22 m to 4.29 m. Their results showed an approximately straight line when voltage and distance were plotted on logarithmic scales. The measurements 
are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bg-a10} (Appendix~\ref{app:bg-figures}).

The authors created a separate calibration curve for each transmitter because the measured signal could differ between transmitters. These curves
were then used to estimate distance from the received voltage.

The model train system will use smaller transmitters moving above fixed receivers. Since the coil arrangement and distances are different the system
will need its own calibration.





\subsubsection{Trilateration and Nonlinear Least Squares}
\label{sec:bg-1-5-1-2}

Trilateration is a method used to find an unknown position using distances to known reference points.

For 2D positioning the unknown coordinates are $(x,y)$. The distance to a reference coil at the
known position $(x_i,y_i)$ can be calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    d_i = \sqrt{(x-x_i)^2+(y-y_i)^2}
\end{equation}

where $d_i$ is the distance between the unknown position and reference coil $i$.
At least three reference points are normally used to find a unique position in 2D. Their placement
is important for accurate positioning.

The distances are first estimated from the received voltages using calibration. These estimates can
contain measurement errors. This means that the distances may not point to exactly the same position.

De Angelis et al. \cite{deangelis2015indoor} used nonlinear least squares to solve this problem.
The method calculates distances from possible coordinates $(x,y)$ to the known reference points.
It then compares these calculated distances with the distances estimated from calibration.
The best position is the one with the smallest total squared difference.

In the model train system the reference coils will be fixed receivers under the table.
The transmitters will move above them. If the height difference between the coils is known as $h$ then the actual distance between their centres 
can be calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    r_i = \sqrt{(x-x_i)^2+(y-y_i)^2+h^2}
\end{equation}

where $r_i$ is the actual distance between the coil centres and $h$ is their vertical separation.





\subsection{Magnetic Field-Based Estimation} \label{sec:bg-1-5-4}
 \subsubsection{Method 1, using Two Nearby Transmitters}

Sheinker et al. \cite{sheinker2013localization} presented a method to find the position of a receiver using two transmitting coils placed close to each other. 
The first transmitter is oriented along the x-axis and the second along the y-axis. Both transmitters have the same known magnetic moment. 
They also operate at different frequencies so the receiver can measure their magnetic fields separately. The arrangement is 
shown in Figure A.12 (Appendix A).

The algorithm uses magnetic field measurements from the first transmitter in the x- and y-directions. These measurements are denoted 
by \(B_{1x}\) and \(B_{1y}\). Their ratio is calculated as:

\[
a_1=\frac{B_{1x}}{B_{1y}}
\]

The magnetic dipole model is then used to calculate \(k_1\) from this ratio. This gives a relationship between the unknown receiver coordinates:

\[
k_1=\frac{3a_1\pm\sqrt{9a_1^2+8}}{4},\qquad x=k_1y
\]

Next, the algorithm uses measurements from both transmitters. It subtracts the y-component of the second transmitter's field from the x-component of 
the first transmitter's field. The result is combined with \(k_1\) to calculate the y-coordinate:

\[
y=\left[
\frac{3\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}M_1(k_1^2-1)}
{(B_{1x}-B_{2y})(k_1^2+1)^{5/2}}
\right]^{1/3}
\]

Here \(M_1\) is the known magnetic moment of the transmitters and \(\mu_0\) is the permeability of free space. 
The method is sensitive to measurement noise when the receiver is close to the diagonal line \(x=y\).

\subsubsection{Method 2, using Two Spaced Transmitters}

In Method 2 the authors use two transmitters placed at different known positions. Both transmitters are oriented in the same direction. The first is placed at $(0,0)$ and the second
at $(0,-R)$. Here R is the known distance between them. The arrangement is also shown in Figure A.12 (Appendix A).

The algorithm starts by calculating the ratio between these components:

\[
a_1=\frac{B_{1x}}{B_{1y}},\qquad
a_3=\frac{B_{3x}}{B_{3y}}
\]

These ratios are used to calculate \(k_1\) and \(k_3\):

\[
k_1=\frac{3a_1\pm\sqrt{9a_1^2+8}}{4}
\]

\[
k_3=\frac{3a_3\pm\sqrt{9a_3^2+8}}{4}
\]

Each value gives a relationship between the unknown receiver coordinates. The first transmitter gives \(x=k_1y\). The second gives \(x=k_3(y+R)\).

The algorithm combines these relationships using the known distance R. The receiver coordinates are then calculated as:

\[
y=\frac{Rk_3}{k_1-k_3},\qquad x=k_1y
\]

Unlike Method 1 this method does not require the exact magnetic moment of the transmitters. This is because the calculations use ratios of magnetic 
field components.




\section{Key Points and Summary} \label{sec:bg-1-6}
The background analysis explains how electromagnetic signals can be used to identify and locate moving transmitter coils. 
Faraday's law explains how a changing magnetic field induces voltage in a receiver coil. The magnetic dipole model and mutual inductance 
show how the received signal depends on coil size, distance and orientation.
The studies show that resonance can make the received signal stronger but can also reduce the available frequency bandwidth. 
It also shows how DFT and lock-in detection can be used to identify different transmitters. For positioning, the studies present 
two methods: voltage-based distance estimation with trilateration and magnetic field-based positioning. Both studies reported good 
positioning results. With this, we can use the findings from these studies when developing our system.
